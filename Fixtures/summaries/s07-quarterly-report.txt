Harbor Bikes Q3 2026 Business Review
Prepared for the leadership team, October 28, 2026

Summary
Harbor Bikes had its strongest third quarter on record. Revenue was $14.2 million, up 18% from the same quarter last year and 6% above plan. Growth came almost entirely from e-bikes and the new subscription service, while sales of traditional bikes fell. Gross margin improved to 41%, from 37% a year ago, mainly because we now assemble e-bikes in-house. Operating costs rose faster than planned because of the new Denver store and higher shipping rates. Net profit was $1.1 million, compared with $0.6 million in Q3 2025.

Three things need decisions from this group: whether to open a second Colorado store in 2027, how to respond to the battery supply risk described in section 5, and whether to keep the subscription price at $59 a month.

Revenue by product line
E-bikes brought in $8.3 million, 58% of revenue, up from 44% a year ago. The Coastline commuter model was the best seller, with 2,140 units sold. The cargo e-bike launched in July sold 610 units, well above the plan of 400, and families with young children make up most of its buyers.

Traditional bikes brought in $3.1 million, down 11% from last year. Road bikes fell the most, by 19%. Kids' bikes held steady. We expect this decline to continue and have cut next year's order of road bikes by a quarter.

Parts, accessories and repairs brought in $1.6 million, up 4%. Repairs grew fastest, at 15%, because more e-bike owners are bringing their bikes in for battery checks and software updates.

The subscription service, Harbor Ride, brought in $1.2 million in its third full quarter. Subscribers pay $59 a month for an e-bike, servicing and theft insurance. We ended the quarter with 7,020 subscribers, up from 4,300 at the end of Q2.

Harbor Ride subscriptions
Harbor Ride is growing faster than any part of the business, but it is also where we are least sure of the economics. Each subscription bike costs us about $1,450 to buy and prepare, so a subscriber needs to stay for about 25 months before the bike pays for itself. So far, monthly cancellations are 3.4%, which implies an average stay of about 29 months. That is only just enough.

Most cancellations happen in the first three months. Exit surveys say the top reasons are moving away (31%), finding the bike too heavy to carry upstairs (22%), and price (18%). The product team is testing a lighter frame for the next model year.

The pricing question: raising the price to $69 a month would make each subscriber profitable after about 21 months, but customer research suggests sign-ups would fall by 15% to 20%. Finance recommends keeping $59 for new subscribers until at least March and testing $65 in one city first. Marketing would prefer a cheaper annual plan instead.

Stores
We have nine stores. Same-store sales grew 9%. The Denver store opened on July 18 and reached break-even in its third month, faster than any store before it. It sold more cargo e-bikes than any other store, which we think is because of Denver's new bike lane network.

The Portland store continues to underperform. Its sales fell 6%, and its lease ends in June 2027. The store team recommends moving to a smaller site near the waterfront; the real estate team has found two options and will report in December.

Based on Denver's results, the store team proposes a second Colorado store in Boulder in spring 2027. Opening a store costs about $900,000 including stock. The team expects it to break even within six months.

Online sales
Online sales were $3.9 million, 27% of revenue, roughly the same share as last year. The new bike finder quiz launched in August raised the share of visitors who buy from 1.8% to 2.3%. Returns of online orders fell to 4% after we added a size guide with video.

Shipping costs rose 14% after our main carrier raised its prices in July. We have started talks with a second carrier and expect to save about $180,000 a year from January.

Supply chain and the battery risk
All of our e-bike batteries come from one supplier, Voltaic Cells, in Malaysia. In September, Voltaic told us that a fire at one of its plants will cut its output by about 30% until March. We currently have eleven weeks of batteries in stock. If nothing changes, we could run short in late January, which is the start of our spring selling season.

There are three options. We could pay a 12% premium to Voltaic for priority supply, costing about $400,000 over six months. We could qualify a second supplier in South Korea, which would take four to five months and cost about $150,000 in testing. Or we could do both. Operations recommends doing both: the premium covers the gap, and the second supplier reduces the risk for the future.

People
We ended the quarter with 214 employees, up from 188. Most new hires were in the Denver store and in assembly. Staff turnover in the stores fell to 21% a year from 29%, which we think is because of the pay rise in April. The engagement survey score was 74, up from 68.

We are struggling to hire e-bike mechanics. Four positions have been open for more than three months. HR has started a paid apprenticeship with the Denver Community College, and the first eight apprentices start in January.

Outlook for Q4
We expect Q4 revenue of $12.5 million to $13 million. Q4 is always slower than Q3 because of the weather, but the holiday season helps accessories and kids' bikes. The forecast assumes no battery shortage before January.

Our main goals for Q4 are to reach 9,000 Harbor Ride subscribers, to sign the second shipping carrier, and to decide on the Boulder store and the battery plan.

Decisions needed
1. Approve a Boulder store opening in spring 2027 (budget $900,000).
2. Approve the battery plan: the priority premium with Voltaic and qualifying a second supplier ($550,000 in total).
3. Agree to keep Harbor Ride at $59 a month for new subscribers until March, with a $65 test in one city.
