How I grew 200 pounds of vegetables on a 10-by-30-foot plot
By Martin Okoye
Published October 30, 2026
12 min read

When I took over plot 47 at the Riverbend allotments three years ago, it was mostly couch grass, broken glass and a rusted bath. Last season the same strip of ground, ten feet wide and thirty feet long, gave my family a little over 200 pounds of vegetables. I weighed every harvest on a kitchen scale and wrote it in a notebook, partly out of pride and partly because I wanted to know what actually worked.

This is not a guide to gardening in general. It is what I did on one small plot, in clay soil, in a wet northern climate, with about five hours a week to spare. Some of it will not apply to you. But I hope the numbers are useful, because most of the advice I read when I started had none.

Start with the soil, not the seeds

My first year was a failure. I planted straight into the clay and most of the seedlings rotted or were eaten by slugs. In the autumn of that year, I dug in 12 wheelbarrow loads of well-rotted horse manure, which the stables down the road gave away for free, and covered the whole plot with cardboard for the winter.

By spring the cardboard had mostly broken down and the worms had pulled the manure into the top few inches of soil. I have not dug the plot since. Each autumn I add a two-inch layer of compost on top and leave it. This "no-dig" method has done more for my harvests than anything else. The soil drains better, there are far fewer weeds, and it takes about half the time.

Raised beds and paths

I split the plot into four beds, each four feet wide, with narrow paths of wood chip between them. Four feet is the widest bed you can reach the middle of without stepping on it. Never stepping on the beds keeps the soil loose.

I did not build wooden sides. Edged beds look tidy, but slugs love to hide under the boards, and the wood cost would have been around $300. My beds are simply mounded soil, and they have worked fine.

What I grow, and what I gave up on

After three years I grow only what does well here and what my family actually eats. The biggest winners by weight last year were potatoes (62 pounds from two short rows), courgettes (38 pounds from just three plants), runner beans (27 pounds), and onions (18 pounds).

I gave up on sweetcorn, which never ripened before the first frost, and on cauliflower, which took the space of a whole bed for months and gave me four small heads. Carrots were a constant fight with carrot fly until I grew them under fine mesh netting, after which they gave 14 pounds.

Tomatoes deserve a mention. Outdoors they caught blight two years running. Last year I grew a blight-resistant variety called Crimson Crush and kept the plants under a simple plastic roof open at the sides. I picked 21 pounds before the end of September.

Succession sowing

The single biggest change in my second year was sowing little and often. Instead of sowing a whole row of lettuce in April and having forty lettuces at once in June, I sow a short row every three weeks from March to August. The same goes for radishes, beetroot and spinach.

It means a few minutes of sowing almost every weekend, but the beds are never empty and we eat salad from May to October. I also start most seeds in modules on a windowsill and plant them out as small plants, which gets them past the slugs' favourite stage.

Dealing with slugs without poison

Slugs were the main reason my first year failed. I do not use slug pellets, partly because of the hedgehogs that live along the railway line. What has worked is a mix of things: going out with a torch on wet evenings twice a week and collecting slugs into a bucket, keeping the grass around the plot short, and sinking beer traps into the soil near young plants.

I also made a small pond from an old washing-up bowl. Within a year it had frogs, and frogs eat slugs. I cannot prove the pond made the difference, but slug damage was noticeably lower the following summer.

Watering

Our council turns off the allotment taps from November to March, and in the summer they are often in heavy use. I collect rain from the shed roof into two 50-gallon water butts, which is enough for about two weeks of dry weather. I water deeply twice a week rather than a little every day, which encourages deeper roots. Wood chip mulch around the courgettes and beans also helps keep the soil moist.

Costs and time

I kept track of what I spent last year. The plot rent is $60 a year. Seeds and seed potatoes came to about $85, netting and mesh $40, and the water butts were a one-off $70. The manure and wood chip were free. That is roughly $185 for the year, not counting the water butts. At supermarket prices, my 200 pounds of vegetables would have cost around $430.

I spent an average of five hours a week on the plot between March and October, and much less in winter. Most of that time was harvesting, which hardly feels like work.

What I would tell someone starting out

Start smaller than you think. Cover half your plot for the first year and grow on the other half. Feed the soil before anything else. Grow what you like to eat. And weigh your harvests: the numbers will tell you what is worth the space.

Next year I want to try growing garlic over the winter and to build a second pond. I will report back.
