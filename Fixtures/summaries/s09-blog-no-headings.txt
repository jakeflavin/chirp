I sold my car eighteen months ago, and I have been meaning to write about it ever since. People keep asking how it is going, usually with the tone you would use for someone who has given up sugar. The honest answer is that it has gone better than I expected in most ways and worse in a couple of ways I did not see coming.

Some background first. I live in a mid-sized city with decent buses, a train line into the centre, and hills that are steep enough to make you think twice. I work from home three days a week and in an office twenty minutes away the other two. I have two children, aged nine and twelve, and a partner who kept her car. So this is not a story about a family going car-free. It is a story about one adult in a two-car household deciding one car was enough.

The money was the reason I did it. When I added it up, my car was costing me about $640 a month: the loan, insurance, fuel, parking at the office, and an average of repairs over the previous three years. I sold it for $14,500, paid off the remaining loan of $6,200, and put the rest in savings. In the eighteen months since, I have spent about $1,900 on transport in total: a monthly bus and train pass at $78, an electric bike I bought second-hand for $900, and a handful of taxis and car-share rentals. Even counting the bike, that is a saving of roughly $9,600 over the period.

The e-bike turned out to be the real change. I bought it thinking I would use it now and then. I now ride it almost every day, including to the office in most weather. The hills that made cycling miserable on a normal bike barely register. My commute by bike takes 24 minutes, which is four minutes longer than driving used to take once you count finding a parking space. I arrive feeling awake rather than irritated. Over the winter I bought proper waterproof trousers and lights, which made the dark mornings bearable.

I was worried about the children. School is two miles away, and in the old routine I drove them most days. Now they take the school bus in the morning, which they were embarrassed about for about a week, and I collect the younger one on the bike with a child seat on the days she has after-school clubs. My twelve-year-old has started cycling to his friends' houses on his own, which he loves and I find nerve-racking. On balance I think the children are more independent than they would have been.

The things I did not expect were mostly about time and planning. Without a car, every trip needs a moment's thought. A big supermarket shop, which used to be a quick trip, now either happens in my partner's car or gets delivered. Delivery costs $5 a time and we use it about twice a month. Visiting my parents, who live ninety minutes away by car and have no public transport near them, has become harder. We now go together as a family in the other car, which means we go less often, and that is the part of this I feel worst about.

There were also a few genuinely bad days. Twice the bus simply did not come and I was late for meetings. Once the e-bike's battery died halfway home in the rain, and I pushed a heavy bike up a hill for twenty minutes. I now charge it every night whether it needs it or not.

Health is the other big change, though I did not do this for my health. I am riding about 60 miles a week. I have lost nine pounds without trying, my resting heart rate dropped from 72 to 61, and I sleep better. My doctor was more excited about this than about anything else I have told her in ten years.

Would I recommend it? It depends almost entirely on where you live and whether there is another car in the household. If you live somewhere flat or can afford an e-bike, if your work is reachable by bus or train, and if someone nearby has a car for the rare big trip, I think most people would be surprised how little they miss it. If your family is spread out across a rural area, it would be much harder. For us, the plan is to stay with one car for at least another two years, and to see whether the second car can go too when it needs replacing.
