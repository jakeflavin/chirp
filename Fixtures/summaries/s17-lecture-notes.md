## What elasticity measures

Price elasticity of demand measures how much the quantity people buy changes when the price changes. It is calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.

Example from class: when a coffee shop raised the price of a latte from $4.00 to $4.40 (a 10% rise), sales fell from 500 to 450 a day (a 10% fall). Elasticity = -10% / 10% = -1. By convention we usually drop the minus sign and say the elasticity is 1.

## Elastic, inelastic and unit elastic

- **Elastic** (greater than 1): quantity changes by more than the price. Restaurant meals, airline tickets for holidays, and particular brands of cereal are examples.
- **Inelastic** (less than 1): quantity changes by less than the price. Petrol in the short run, insulin, and cigarettes are examples.
- **Unit elastic** (exactly 1): quantity changes by the same percentage as the price, like the latte example.

The professor stressed that elasticity is not the same as the slope of the demand curve. Along a straight-line demand curve, elasticity changes: demand is elastic at high prices and inelastic at low prices.

## What makes demand more elastic

1. **Substitutes.** The more close substitutes there are, the more elastic demand is. Demand for "Pepsi" is far more elastic than demand for "soft drinks".
2. **Share of income.** Goods that take a big share of your budget (cars, rent) have more elastic demand than cheap ones (salt, matches).
3. **Necessity or luxury.** Necessities tend to be inelastic.
4. **Time.** Demand becomes more elastic over time, because people find substitutes. After a petrol price rise, people keep driving at first, but over a few years they buy smaller cars or move closer to work.

## Elasticity and total revenue

This is the part that will be on the exam. Total revenue is price times quantity.

- If demand is **elastic**, raising the price **lowers** total revenue, because the fall in quantity outweighs the higher price.
- If demand is **inelastic**, raising the price **raises** total revenue.
- If demand is **unit elastic**, total revenue does not change.

In the latte example, revenue was $2,000 a day before ($4.00 × 500) and $1,980 after ($4.40 × 450), almost unchanged, which fits an elasticity close to 1.

Real case discussed: in 2019 a city transit system raised fares by 25% and ridership fell by only 8%, so fare revenue went up. Transit demand in the short run is inelastic because many riders have no other way to get to work.

## Taxes and who pays them

When a tax is placed on a good, the burden falls mostly on whichever side of the market is less elastic. Because demand for cigarettes is inelastic, most of a cigarette tax is paid by smokers through higher prices, not by tobacco companies.

## Cross-price and income elasticity (brief)

- Cross-price elasticity: how demand for one good responds to the price of another. Positive for substitutes (tea and coffee), negative for complements (printers and ink).
- Income elasticity: how demand responds to income. Positive for normal goods, negative for inferior goods such as instant noodles.
