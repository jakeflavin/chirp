Lighthouse automation in Scotland was the gradual replacement of resident lighthouse keepers with automatic equipment, carried out by the Northern Lighthouse Board between 1960 and 1998. By the end of the programme, all of the Board's lighthouses were monitored remotely from its headquarters in Edinburgh, and the profession of lighthouse keeper had ended in Scotland.

Background
For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, every major Scottish lighthouse was staffed by a team of keepers, usually three, who worked in shifts to keep the lamp burning, wind the clockwork that turned the lens, and record the weather. Remote stations on rocks far from land, such as the Bell Rock and Muckle Flugga, were "rock stations" where keepers lived without their families, working rotations of about four weeks on and four weeks off. Shore stations had houses where keepers lived with their families.

The work was isolating but steady. In 1950 the Board employed around 400 keepers across more than 80 lighthouses.

Early automation
The first automatic lights in Scotland were small unwatched beacons lit by acetylene gas, used from the early 1900s on minor sites. Automating major lights only became practical in the 1960s, when reliable electric lamps, automatic lamp changers and fog detectors became available.

The first major station to be automated was Little Ross, in Kirkcudbrightshire, in 1960. The change there was hastened by a tragedy: in August 1960 one keeper was found shot dead, and his colleague was later convicted of his murder.

The automation programme
During the 1970s and 1980s, the Board automated stations steadily, usually a few each year. Automation typically involved converting the lamp to mains electricity or diesel generators, fitting an automatic lamp changer that replaced a failed bulb, and installing telemetry so that faults could be reported to a monitoring centre.

The programme saved money. A Board report in 1982 estimated that each automated station cost about one fifth as much to run as a staffed one. It also reflected changes in shipping, as satellite navigation reduced the importance of lighthouses for large ships.

Keepers and their families were offered other jobs with the Board, redundancy payments or early retirement. Many former keepers stayed on as "attendants", part-time local staff who visit a lighthouse regularly to check the equipment.

The last keepers
The last staffed lighthouse in Scotland was Fair Isle South, between Orkney and Shetland. Its keepers left on 31 March 1998, ending more than two hundred years of resident keepers in Scotland. A ceremony was held in Edinburgh and attended by the Duke of Edinburgh.

Legacy
Many former keepers' houses have been sold and turned into holiday homes, museums or private houses. Several lighthouses, including Kinnaird Head in Fraserburgh, now house museums about the keepers' lives. Oral history projects have recorded the memories of former keepers and their families.
