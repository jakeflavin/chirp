# Chapter Seven: The Lighthouse Keeper's Ledger

The ferry dropped Nora at the harbour at four o'clock, an hour later than the timetable promised, and by then the light was already going. She stood on the slipway with her one suitcase and watched the boat turn back towards the mainland, its wake spreading white across the grey water. Nobody had come to meet her. She had not really expected anyone to.

The island was smaller than it had looked on the map. A single road ran from the harbour up the hill, past a pub, a shop with a post office counter, and a row of eight cottages painted in colours that had faded to the same pale grey. At the top of the hill stood the lighthouse, white and squat, and beside it the keeper's house where her great-aunt Margaret had lived for forty-one years.

Margaret had died in March. The letter from the solicitor had taken five weeks to reach Nora in Glasgow, and it had said only that she was the sole heir, that the house was hers, and that there was "a matter of some papers" that Margaret had asked be given to her in person.

She walked up the hill. The wind came off the sea in long cold pushes and she had to lean into it. Outside the pub, an old man in a yellow oilskin was mending a lobster pot, and he looked up as she passed.

"You'll be Margaret's niece," he said. It was not a question.

"Great-niece. Nora."

"Duncan Reid." He did not offer his hand, which was covered in tar. "She talked about you. Said you were the only one of them who ever wrote back."

Nora did not know what to say to that. She had written to Margaret twice a year since she was eleven, short dutiful letters at Christmas and on Margaret's birthday, and Margaret had always replied at length, in a small slanting hand, with news of storms and seabirds and the comings and goings of boats. Nora had kept every letter. She had not visited once.

"The key's under the stone by the door," Duncan said. "Fiona from the shop's been airing the place. She left you milk and bread."

The keeper's house smelled of damp wool and woodsmoke. Fiona had lit the stove, and the kitchen was warm. On the table was a loaf, a bottle of milk, a box of matches, and a brown envelope with Nora's name on it in the solicitor's typing. Inside was a small iron key and a note: "For the desk in the lamp room. M.D."

Nora climbed the lighthouse in the last of the daylight. There were one hundred and twelve steps, worn into shallow dips in the middle, and at the top a narrow room below the great lamp itself, which had not been lit by hand since the light was automated in 1998. Against the wall stood a heavy oak desk. The key fitted the bottom drawer.

Inside were ledgers. Twenty-three of them, bound in green cloth, numbered on the spine. Nora opened the first one and found not the weather records she expected but a list of names, dates and boats, in Margaret's hand, beginning in September 1984. "Sea Sparrow, 2 a.m., three aboard, landed at the north cove. Taken to the Reids'." Then, a week later: "Kittiwake, no lights, four aboard, one child. Sent on to Oban on the Tuesday ferry."

She read for an hour by the light of her phone. The entries went on for years. Boats arriving at night without lights, people landing at the north cove where no one could see from the village, people taken in by the Reids, by Fiona's mother, by the minister, and sent on a few days later by ferry. There were no explanations. Some entries had a single word added later in a different ink: "Safe." A few had nothing.

She was halfway through the fourth ledger when she heard the door at the bottom of the stairs. Footsteps came up slowly, with pauses. It was Duncan, out of breath, holding a torch.

"Saw the light," he said. "Thought you might have found them."

"What is this?"

Duncan lowered himself onto the bottom step of the ladder to the lamp. He was quiet for a long time.

"People who needed to get somewhere without anyone asking questions," he said finally. "Women, mostly, at the start. Getting away from men who'd hurt them. Your aunt knew a woman in Belfast who'd send them over. Later it was others. Anyone who needed it. The island looked after them for a few nights and sent them on."

"For how long?"

"Till she couldn't do the stairs any more. Two years ago." He looked at the ledgers. "Five hundred and some, she reckoned. She wrote them all down so that somebody would know, after. She said you'd know what to do with them."

Nora looked at the green spines on the desk. Twenty-three books. She thought about her letters, two a year, about storms and exams and her flat in Glasgow, and Margaret's long replies about seabirds, and how none of it had ever said anything at all.

"I don't know what to do with them," she said.

"No," said Duncan. "She didn't either, at first." He stood up, stiffly. "Come down to the pub tomorrow. There's some people you should meet. Some of them came off those boats."

When he had gone, Nora sat in the lamp room until the stars came out over the sea. Then she opened the fifth ledger and kept reading.
